The PC component market has changed drastically since late 2025, especially with the growing demand for AI. With companies such as Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle investing billions in AI infrastructure and data centers, the demand for memory and storage has increased significantly. As manufacturers prioritize supplying data centers, Micron has even exited its Crucial consumer business. As a result, prices for components like RAM and SSDs have skyrocketed, making what was once a relatively affordable way to get into PC gaming a lot more expensive.

Despite all of this, I've been wanting to upgrade my PC from AM4 to AM5 for quite some time. I knew component prices were bad, but I was still committed to upgrading and building something exactly how I wanted it.

## My Previous PCs

I turned 16 during COVID and got my first job as a pizza cook. I bought an NZXT prebuilt with the money I earned working for $13 an hour. Before that, I had an office PC that could run Fortnite and CS:GO. I think it had a GeForce GTX 970 or something like that. The NZXT came with an Intel Core i5, an RTX 2060, and 16GB of DDR4 RAM.

Over time, I upgraded that NZXT prebuilt by switching to AMD with a Ryzen 7 5700X, upgrading the GPU to an RTX 4060 Ti, increasing my RAM to 32GB, and adding a 2TB SSD.

At that point, it was practically a brand-new PC. This became my main PC for a while until I finally decided to make the jump to AM5 and build something new.

## Why I Decided to Upgrade

I've been wanting to make the upgrade from AM4 to AM5 for a while now, but I just kept putting it off. My previous PC was fine, so I never really felt the need to rush into upgrading. However, after my girlfriend and I moved into our own place, I finally decided it was time to build a new PC, especially since I noticed that prices might go up even more.

One of my biggest reasons for upgrading was wanting something more future-proof that I wouldn't have to worry about upgrading again for several years. I wanted a PC that could handle everything from experimenting with AI and running large language models (LLMs) locally to playing any new game releases (we're all patiently waiting for GTA 6 on PC).

This time around, I wanted to build a high-end PC and pick every component myself. Rather than going with another prebuilt, I picked out each part and built something I'd envisioned.

## My New PC Specs

After spending some time looking at different components, I finally settled on the parts I wanted for my new build. My main goal was to build something that could handle pretty much anything I threw at it while also being a system I wouldn't need to upgrade for several years.

Here are the components I ended up going with:

| Component | Model |
| --- | --- |
| CPU | AMD Ryzen 7 9850X3D |
| GPU | ASUS TUF Gaming RTX 5070 Ti 16GB |
| RAM | 32GB DDR5-6000 CL36 |
| Storage | SN850X 4TB NVMe SSD |

![PC Parts](/images/pc_parts.jpg)

I got my CPU, RAM, and motherboard as a combo from Micro Center. It was a 3-in-1 bundle that was too good to pass up. I wanted something that could handle my everyday workload and also deliver excellent gaming performance. I went with the RTX 5070 Ti instead of the 5080 because I saved about $600 and didn't care about the 10% to 15% performance difference. I mainly play competitive games such as Deadlock, CS2, and LoL, so I run all of those games on low settings anyway for the best performance and to maintain high FPS. I mainly cared about having 16GB of VRAM for running local LLMs and other experiments with AI.

In total, I ended up spending $3,020.25 on the entire build from Micro Center. Definitely not cheap, especially with the current component prices, but I built something I'd wanted for a long time and will be happy with for the next few years.

## Are Prebuilts a Better Option Now?

Building your own PC has traditionally been the cheaper option compared with buying a prebuilt. However, times have changed. With the rising prices of PC components, that isn't always the case anymore. Some prebuilts can actually be cheaper than buying all the components individually.

A good example is my friend. He was looking to upgrade his PC and recently purchased a PowerSpec G763 from Micro Center after I recommended it to him. It's pretty similar to my PC. It has a Ryzen 7 9800X3D, an RTX 5070 Ti, 32GB of DDR5 RAM, and a 2TB SSD for just **$2,299.99** (it was on sale, so good timing on his part). Meanwhile, I spent **$3,020.25** on my custom build, a difference of $720.26.

![Friend's Prebuilt](/images/friends_prebuild_specs.jpg)

Our PCs are similar, which shows how competitive prebuilt prices can be. Personally, I prefer building my own PC because I get to choose exactly which parts I use.

## Wrapping Up

This doesn't mean you shouldn't build your own PC. Even with the rising prices of PC components, I'm really happy with how my build turned out, and I believe it was worth it. Whether you decide to build your own or buy a prebuilt, I think both are great options depending on your needs and budget.
